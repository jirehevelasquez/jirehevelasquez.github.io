Compressor Rotor Joints and Interference Fit Study was a mechanical engineering senior project sponsored by Solar Turbines. The project investigated how radial interference fits between compressor rotor plates, dowel pins, and pilot joints affect overall rotor assembly stiffness and vibration behavior.

Overview

Solar Turbines uses stacked rotor plates in compressor assemblies, where plates are joined with interference pilot fits and dowel pins. These joints can influence rotor stiffness and vibration behavior. The engineering objective was to determine how changes in radial interference fit dimensions and pilot ledge geometry affect natural frequency, which was used as a measurable indicator of assembly stiffness.

The team developed a scaled rotor plate assembly that preserved the key contact mechanics of the real compressor interface while remaining manufacturable and testable using Cal Poly laboratory equipment. Three configurations were studied: a low-interference configuration, a higher-interference/longer-pilot configuration, and a welded configuration used as a high-stiffness reference case.

Problem / Goal

The problem was that radial shifting between compressor rotor plates under operating loads could affect compressor stability and vibration response. Because rotor dynamic behavior is strongly tied to stiffness, Solar Turbines needed a way to understand which joint parameters most influenced assembly stiffness: dowel pin interference, pilot fit interference, or pilot ledge contact length.

The project goal was to create an ANSYS-based study tool and validate it with physical testing. The model needed to predict changes in natural frequency across multiple interference-fit configurations, and the experimental results needed to confirm whether the simulation trends were meaningful.

My Role and Impact

My work contributed to the full design-build-test loop of the project. I helped support the analytical modeling workflow, CAD and drawing preparation, prototype manufacturing plan, testing process, and interpretation of ANSYS-versus-experimental results. The value of the project came from connecting the simulation results to real hardware behavior, so my work focused on helping the team move from a theoretical interference-fit study to a validated physical experiment.

  • Supported CAD development for scaled rotor plate configurations and manufacturing-ready part geometry.
  • Helped organize CAD files for the male/female plate configurations and welded reference configuration.
  • Contributed to ANSYS modeling decisions for contact stress, preloaded modal analysis, and natural frequency extraction.
  • Helped evaluate design variables including dowel pin interference, pilot fit interference, and pilot ledge contact length.
  • Supported manufacturing planning for CNC machining, CMM inspection, press-fit assembly, and welded configuration preparation.
  • Participated in modal impact hammer testing and comparison between experimental frequency peaks and ANSYS mode shapes.
  • Helped interpret the final trend: pilot ledge length had a much stronger effect on stiffness than increasing interference alone.

Engineering Process

The project followed a complete engineering process: define the rotor joint problem, identify key design variables, build a parameterized ANSYS model, select manufacturable configurations, produce CAD and drawings, fabricate prototypes, inspect critical dimensions, perform modal impact testing, and compare test results with simulation predictions.

Because full-scale compressor hardware was too large and complex for the available test equipment, the team designed a scaled assembly. The prototype used two circular plates joined by a central pilot interface and dowel pins. This simplified geometry allowed the team to isolate the effects of radial interference and pilot ledge contact length without introducing unnecessary manufacturing and testing variables.

Technical Work

ANSYS Parametric Study

The ANSYS model was built to study how interference-fit parameters affected assembly stiffness. The analysis workflow used contact stress and modal analysis to estimate natural frequencies for different design points. Because mass remained approximately constant between configurations, changes in natural frequency were used to compare changes in effective stiffness.

  • Modeled two rotor plates, dowel pins, pilot interfaces, and contact regions.
  • Used parameterized design variables for dowel pin interference, pilot fit interference, and pilot ledge length.
  • Applied contact definitions to represent interference-induced preload between mating surfaces.
  • Performed modal analysis to extract natural frequencies and compare predicted stiffness trends.
  • Used simulation results to decide which configurations were worth manufacturing and testing.

CAD and Prototype Design

The physical validation assemblies were designed as scaled rotor plate prototypes. Each interference configuration used a male and female plate with pilot ledges and dowel-pin holes. The welded configuration served as a reference case where the two plates were joined into a rigid assembly.

  • Created male and female rotor plate CAD for two interference-fit configurations.
  • Created welded male/female plate geometry for the reference configuration.
  • Used SolidWorks and Fusion 360 files to support manufacturing and revision control.
  • Designed geometry to remain feasible for CNC machining, inspection, press fitting, and testing.
  • Included mounting/test features needed for fixed-free and free-free modal testing workflows.

Manufacturing and Assembly

The prototype manufacturing process required precision because small dimensional changes directly affected the intended interference fits. The team used CNC machining, CMM inspection, dowel-pin pressing, and plate pressing operations to create the physical assemblies.

  • Machined rotor plates from 1018 steel round stock using CNC milling operations.
  • Used soft jaws and multiple setups to hold the circular plate geometry during machining.
  • Measured critical dimensions with CMM inspection to verify interference-related features.
  • Pressed dowel pins into the male plates and assembled the interference-fit configurations.
  • Created a welded configuration to act as a high-stiffness baseline for comparison.

Modal Impact Testing

After manufacturing, the team used modal impact hammer testing to validate the simulation results. The assemblies were excited with an impact hammer while accelerometers measured the dynamic response. The resulting frequency response data was used to identify natural frequencies and compare them with the ANSYS-predicted mode shapes.

  • Used impact hammer testing to excite the rotor plate assemblies.
  • Used accelerometers and a spectrum analyzer to identify natural frequency peaks.
  • Tested configurations under fixed-free and free-free boundary conditions.
  • Performed stationary accelerometer/hammer, roving hammer/fixed accelerometer, and roving accelerometer/fixed hammer tests.
  • Compared experimental natural frequencies against ANSYS predictions to evaluate model validity.

Results / Status

The project successfully produced a parameterized ANSYS model, manufactured three physical rotor plate assemblies, and completed modal impact testing. The experimental results generally matched the ANSYS trends, which supported the simulation approach as a useful tool for comparing joint stiffness configurations.

The most important conclusion was that increasing pilot ledge length had the largest effect on increasing natural frequency and overall assembly stiffness. Increasing dowel pin interference and pilot fit interference alone produced smaller stiffness gains within the tested ranges. The welded configuration produced the highest stiffness overall, which made it useful as a reference case for comparing the interference-fit assemblies.

Challenges and Debugging

  • Boundary conditions: the testing method evolved from a planned shake-table setup to modal impact hammer testing because of frequency and load limitations.
  • Manufacturing tolerances: small differences in machined dimensions affected interference fits and contributed to test/model variation.
  • Contact modeling: accurately representing interference-induced preload required nonlinear contact assumptions and careful ANSYS setup.
  • Testing repeatability: accelerometer placement, hammer strike location, suspension conditions, and noise affected the clarity of frequency peaks.
  • Scope control: axial interference was removed from the physical study because realistic axial preload would have required tooling beyond the team’s available resources.

Key Takeaways

This project strengthened my experience with mechanical analysis, manufacturing, and experimental validation. The most valuable part of the project was seeing how simulation results must be connected to real hardware, measurement uncertainty, manufacturing tolerances, and test setup limitations before they can be trusted for engineering decisions.

The main engineering takeaway was that increasing preload magnitude is not always the most effective way to increase assembly stiffness. In this study, increasing the contact length of the pilot ledge produced a stronger stiffness improvement than simply increasing interference values. That result showed the importance of understanding load paths, contact area, and global stiffness rather than focusing only on local fit pressure.
